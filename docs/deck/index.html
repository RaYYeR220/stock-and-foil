First Brands filed for Chapter 11 carrying roughly $2.3 billion of receivables-finance liabilities. The invoices behind them are alleged to have been forged, inflated tenfold, and presented to more than one financier.

Three frauds, one shape: a receivable that is not what the lender was told it was, or is not the lender's to hold. On paper, each is indistinguishable from an honest one — and none of the financiers could have caught it by comparing notes, because there was no way to compare notes that cost less than the fraud.

Both fixes cost more
than the fraud.

There are exactly two ways to check whether a receivable is already financed, and receivables finance has rejected both for the same reason.

Option one

Show the other lender your invoices

An invoice file is a client list and a price list. Handing it to a rival costs more than the fraud does, so every financier keeps its book to itself — and the same receivable gets funded twice.

Option two

Put every fingerprint in one registry

Central fingerprint registries do work, and they ask every financier to trust one operator with the shape of its whole book — who its clients are, how much it lends, and when.

Midnight's dual ledger removes the trade-off. The checks run in zero knowledge against private state, and the public ledger holds only what uniqueness needs: a commitment, a one-time marker, a sealed record.

Four parties, one record.

A receivable passes through four hands. Each hand proves something, and leaves a mark nobody else can read.

Every check runs while the transaction is being built. A fraudulent pledge cannot be proved, so it never reaches the chain at all — there is no bad state to roll back and no failed transaction to read.

Three refusals.

A tally stick was split so that one half could not be forged against the other. These are the same idea, proved rather than whittled. The assert message in the contract is the product's refusal code, and the SDK and the interface carry it through unchanged.

A forged invoice

Financing raised against an invoice the buyer never owed.

NOT_ACKNOWLEDGED

No acknowledgment leaf exists for this invoice, so the offer has no membership proof to make. There is no second half for it to match.

An inflated invoice

The buyer owes $2,300. The financier is shown $23,000.

NOT_ACKNOWLEDGED

The amount is inside what the buyer signed. A $23,000 invoice is a different commitment from the $2,300 on record, and nothing acknowledges it.

A double pledge

One receivable financed by several financiers at once.

ALREADY_ENCUMBERED

The one-time marker for this receivable is already spent. A stick that has been split cannot be split again.

The refused lender learns exactly one bit: this receivable is already encumbered. Not by whom, not for how much, not since when — and the existing pledge is neither touched nor disclosed.

Two registries. One answer.

A pledge marker is a hash of the invoice and its parties alone — no registry identity, no operator key, no deployment salt. So the same receivable has the same marker in every registry, and asking another one is a read of public state, not a business relationship.

“The catch is scale: it only works once many competing financiers join the same registry, a consortium sale that's hard for a startup.” Wave 1 review · Product. Below is the answer, run rather than argued.

Read live from both deployed contracts in the visitor's own tab at stock-and-foil.vercel.app/#check — no proxy, no API key, no wallet. A registry that does not answer prints no answer, never free.

Why the marker travels

These are the keys the two ledgers actually store the entry under, read back from each contract — not one hash computed twice in one process. And it still cannot be guessed: the fingerprint carries the seller's secret salt.

and before any of that

One lender alone is already worth it

A forged and an inflated invoice are refused by the buyer's acknowledgment, not by other lenders. One factoring house running its own registry catches both on day one, plus duplicates across its own branches, with nobody else on the network.

registerPeer is a declaration, not a proof that a peer is honest.

Nobody has to buy
a token first.

Every Midnight transaction pays its fee in DUST; DUST is generated only by registered NIGHT, is non-transferable, and a contract cannot hold any. Taken literally, that means a buyer's accounts-payable clerk acquires a crypto token before confirming that an invoice is real.

The split, and what it costs each side

the official sponsorship pattern

The registry authenticates by knowledge of a secret whose hash is on the ledger — never by who paid the fee. So the sponsor's money buys a block slot and nothing else, and sponsoring somebody weakens no claim the contract makes about them.

The service, and the two reviews it had to pass

sponsor/ · 87 tests

A synchronous budget reservation that holds under twenty parallel requests, so a burst cannot overspend it. A rate-limit bucket keyed by API key or socket address rather than a spoofable X-Forwarded-For. And a suite that asserts what the service is allowed to say: no upstream error text and no balance ever reaches a caller.

Read from the chain before the run — the command aborts unless the balance is exactly zero — and read again afterwards. The claim is not that the wallet ought to have been empty but that it was, at the moment it transacted.

What the ledger shows,
and what it never shows.

Printed on the ledger public

  • Acknowledgment leaves. One per acknowledged invoice. The leaf does not say which.
  • Pledge markers and their status. Offered, pledged, released or settled.
  • Per-pledge holder tags. A fresh tag for every pledge.
  • Sealed records and their ephemeral keys.
  • Certificate floors, pool size and validity.
  • Disclosure requests, cases and approvals.
  • Peer declarations. Which other registries this operator recognises.
  • Settlement amounts and addresses, unshielded.

Settlement is the one exception, and it is published under Honest limits rather than hidden.

Never printed stays with the parties

  • The invoice. Buyer, supplier, number, amount, due date.
  • Which invoice a marker belongs to.
  • Which financier holds which pledge, or how big its book is.
  • The record contents, without 2 of 3 keyholders.
  • The receivables inside a borrowing base.
  • Anything the auditor learns, to anyone else.
  • Who was refused, and what they were trying to pledge.

The supplier salts the invoice before the buyer acknowledges it, so a financier who guesses an invoice still cannot test whether it is encumbered.

The explorer in the app reads the same projection an indexer does — and this band is the whole of it. Counts, markers, statuses, ciphertexts. There is nothing underneath to be careless with.

Disclosure nobody
can do alone.

An insolvency practitioner has to be able to open a specific pledge. One party being able to do that would undo everything else.

the key

A 2-of-3 Shamir sharing of the disclosure key over the Jubjub scalar field. The constructor checks the sharing itself, in point form, and refuses a ceremony that would let one party read everything or no two read anything.

the share

A keyholder's share of the record key is computed inside the circuit and sealed to the auditor's key. No keyholder ever publishes anything another keyholder can use.

the check

The opened record recomputes the invoice fingerprint, the acknowledgment leaf and the pledge marker, so a doctored disclosure fails against the ledger's own value. Request and approvals stay public: no record is read unseen.

the case NEW IN WAVE 2

An insolvency over four receivables used to need eight approvals. openCase names them under one reference and approveCase seals one share per record in a single call — two approvals, not eight. A share is bound to its case, so an approval cannot be replayed against a record the keyholders never saw.

Prove the pool clears the floor.

An asset-based lender advances against a borrowing base. Today it gets a spreadsheet of line items and takes the borrower's word for the rest.

Each locked slot becomes an ordinary offer expiring with the certificate, which the lender takes up one at a time with the same accept it uses for a single invoice — or all at once with acceptBatch, new this wave, which proves membership once for the whole batch and either takes every offer or none. Distinctness and the floor are proved before any slot is locked, so a pool that lists one invoice twice is refused for the duplicate rather than for an encumbrance it caused itself.

Four receivables, and on a real network exactly four

Each proved acknowledged, unencumbered, not yet settled and not overdue — the same four checks a single pledge passes. A partly filled pool passes the simulator and fails on a live network: the proof server rejects the zeroed curve point an unused slot leaves in the public transcript. The lock, and what it does not prove

A certificate that only asserted a total would be a promise. This one writes four pledge markers in the same transaction. What no circuit can check is that those markers are addressed to the lender the certificate names: a holder tag is derived from the lender's own secret, so the contract has nothing to test the seller's claim against. The lender recomputes every tag from public state before money moves.

The supplier's own book, in the app. The registry never holds an invoice — only a hash of the whole document.

The seal

A fresh scalar e per record. E = e·G is published; the mask is Poseidon(S.x, S.y, j) over S = e·disclosurePk. Poseidon because a SHA-derived mask sits below 2248 and would leak the high bits of the identities it pads.

The deploy

Sixteen verifier keys total about 34 kB; one transaction's byte budget takes six. The deploy carries six, then ten VerifierKeyInsert transactions carry the rest — eleven per registry, twenty-two for the two.

The adversarial suite

Sixty-five scenarios — at least one for every one of the 39 refusal codes the contract can raise — plus the lying witness: when no real Merkle path exists the witness hands back a dummy one, so the refusal comes from the circuit's assert and not from a JavaScript exception. That distinction is the test.

The property model

fast-check drives random lifecycles against a reference model. The invariants: at most one active pledge per invoice, a settled invoice is never pledgeable again, and proceeds are paid exactly once to the correct payee.

Negative controls

Threshold decryption is tested with every pair of keyholders — and with one share alone, and with the wrong auditor key, both of which must fail. An opened record that does not recompute the ledger's marker is a failure, not a warning.

The documented leaks

A whole suite reproduces the leaks and griefing vectors the threat model admits, and another asserts value by value what each circuit publishes. "Not defended" is a measured statement rather than an assumption. Every item on slide 16 has a test here.

Honest limits.

Every item below has a test that reproduces it, so "not defended" is a measured statement rather than an assumption. Three of them were found this wave by running the thing on a real network.

  • It has never seen a real invoice. Every run is synthetic: our personas, our numbers. No lender has used this, no pilot has run, and nothing here has been tested against a real book.
  • One registry cannot see another. A ledger cannot read another ledger, so a second registry will accept a receivable already pledged in the first — Harbour did, on preview, at block 1322275. Only the lender's own cross-registry check stops the advance, and registerPeer is a declaration, not a proof that a peer is honest.
  • Collusion still works. A buyer who colludes with a supplier can acknowledge an invoice that does not exist. The registry makes that acknowledgment non-repudiable; it does not prevent it.
  • Settlement is public. The settlement token is unshielded, so amounts and payer and payee addresses are visible at payment time. Contract custody of shielded coins is blocked upstream.
  • The operator is trusted twice. It decides who is admitted, and as the deployer it holds the contract's maintenance authority — a strictly larger power. Production should move that authority to a multi-party key, or burn it.
  • The keys are dealt, not generated. A ceremony splits the disclosure key rather than a distributed protocol, and two colluding keyholders reconstruct it off-chain with no public request at all.
  • Single-record approvals can still be pooled. A keyholder's share is bound to the record, so two requests naming one record with one approval each open it. openCase closes this for an insolvency by binding shares to a case reference; the single-record path is unchanged.
  • A certificate names a lender it cannot bind. The holder tags on its locked slots come from the seller, and no circuit can tie them to that lender. The lender recomputes every tag from public state before it advances.
  • A borrowing base shows its markers, and needs all four slots. Publishing the markers it locks is what makes the lock verifiable. A partly filled pool passes the simulator and fails on a live network: the proof server rejects the zeroed curve point an unused slot leaves behind.
  • A seller can leak its own records. Reusing a sealing scalar makes two records comparable to anyone, and re-offering with the same scalar overwrites the earlier one. The SDK draws a fresh scalar every time; the discipline is still the caller's.
  • A settled, unfinanced book is linkable. An opened record reveals the supplier's id, and the seller payee tag derives from it, so every settled-but-unfinanced receivable of that supplier becomes recognisable without further approval.
  • Timing correlates. An observer can link an acknowledgment to a later offer by when each happened.
  • Sealing rests on Poseidon. The platform does not promise the hash stays stable across upgrades, which is why every record carries a version.
  • Preview, not mainnet. Two registries run on Midnight preview, a public testnet anyone can query. We attempted mainnet three times through a sponsoring wallet: each transaction was proved, balanced and accepted by the node, and each was invalidated in its pool 75 seconds later. Nothing landed, nothing was spent, and the cause we could not exclude is on the sponsor's side of the line — slide 17.
  • Not audited. An internal security review, with findings accepted on the record — one of them, F-08, is what case-scoped disclosure answers. No external audit.

The threat model names the attacker behind each of these, the property it breaks and the test that reproduces it. Both documents ship with the repository. docs/THREAT-MODEL.md · docs/PRIVACY-BOUNDARY.md
